MY FYP, EXPLAINED IN FULL
Smart Campus Facility Reservation System
with AI-Assisted Recommendations
Amaan Rashid | APU School of Engineering
============================================================

A spoken script for explaining the whole project to a
supervisor, second marker or panel. Roughly 13-15 minutes
read at a normal pace. Written to be said out loud, not
read off the page.


------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT I'M BUILDING
------------------------------------------------------------

A Smart Campus Facility Reservation System with AI-Assisted
Recommendations.

One platform for every kind of campus facility - discussion
rooms, badminton courts, event halls - where the
administrator sets the booking rules without needing a
developer, the database itself refuses to double-book, a
student who can't get the time they wanted is offered the
next best options, and questions about availability and
rules get answered in plain language.


------------------------------------------------------------
THE PROBLEM - AND I WANT TO BE CAREFUL HERE
------------------------------------------------------------

My original proposal said campus booking is manual. Paper
forms, counter queues. When I actually checked, that turned
out to be wrong, and I'd rather say so now than have you
find it.

Booking is already online almost everywhere. LibCal alone
runs at seven and a half thousand institutions in a hundred
and six countries. Monash Malaysia uses it. Sunway uses it.

So the problem isn't that booking is undigitised. It's that
digitisation stopped at the transaction. The system records
the request and then does nothing.

    [This next part is the core idea. Slow down.]

Booking a room used to cost you something. You walked to a
counter, in opening hours, and asked a person. That friction
limited how much space any one student could hold.

Online booking removed the friction and put nothing in its
place, because not turning up still costs you nothing.

So booking early, booking long, and booking a room bigger
than you need became the sensible strategy. And when enough
students do the sensible thing, the screen says full over a
building that isn't.

    [Now the evidence.]

Sunway's own booking page - running LibCal, twenty minutes
from here - carries two rules.

It sets a MINIMUM group size of three for a pod that seats
ten. Nobody writes a minimum unless pairs were taking rooms
built for groups.

And it requires students to appear at a ground-floor service
counter within fifteen minutes of the booked time, or
forfeit.

Think about that second one. You book online, at midnight,
from your room. To prove you turned up, a member of staff
has to see your face.

The same fifteen-minute rule appears at Pacific, at Camosun,
at Duke Law.

That is not a criticism of Sunway. It's a reasonable
response to real behaviour. And that's exactly the point -
those are rules written on a web page and enforced by
people, standing in for logic the software doesn't have.


------------------------------------------------------------
AIM AND FIVE OBJECTIVES
------------------------------------------------------------

The aim is one booking system for every kind of campus
facility, making the sharing of campus space visible and
fair, and contributing to at least eight UN-SDGs.

ONE. Design a facility model where the admin sets the rules
once for a TYPE of facility and every room of that type
follows them, with the option to override one room - and
where attendance is recorded, so a slot booked and never
used becomes visible. SDG 11 and 12.

TWO. Build the booking rules and the no-overlap rule into
the database itself rather than the program, so if two
students request the same court at the same moment, only one
succeeds. SDG 9 and 10.

THREE. Construct a recommendation engine that, when the
requested time is gone, offers the best free alternatives
instead of just refusing - scored on how close they are to
the time asked for, how well the room size fits the group,
and how busy that period is - and that learns what each
student accepts. SDG 7 and 3.

FOUR. Develop a chatbot that answers using only the records
read back for that particular student, so it can't invent a
rule or reveal someone else's booking. SDG 4 and 8.

FIVE. Evaluate by measuring how it behaves, not by
describing it.


------------------------------------------------------------
THE LITERATURE
------------------------------------------------------------

Eleven papers, 2021 to 2026, reviewed paper by paper as
instructed - problem, method, outcomes, gaps for each. Five
on campus booking systems, two on recommender systems, four
on chatbots in higher education.

Two of them do most of the work.

Roy and Dutta's systematic review shows collaborative
filtering fails on cold start and sparsity - which is
exactly my situation, with a handful of facilities and no
booking history. That's why I use deterministic scoring with
online weight learning rather than a trained model. It's not
that I couldn't train one. There's nothing to train it on.

Okonkwo and Ade-Ibijola identify incorrect responses as the
core chatbot risk. That's why my assistant can only phrase
facts it retrieved. It never generates a rule.

I also added a section reviewing what universities actually
BUY, because peer-reviewed papers describe what researchers
built, not what institutions run. That matters, because two
of my original gap claims didn't survive it.


------------------------------------------------------------
THE GAP - NARROWED HONESTLY
------------------------------------------------------------

I originally claimed no system has configurable booking
rules. That's false. LibCal has them. Skedda has them.

What IS true is that configurable rules always live inside
one facility segment. The library buys LibCal, the sports
centre buys Fusion, the registrar buys 25Live. Nothing spans
them, because each product's data model was built around one
class of facility. City University of Hong Kong publishes
this openly: rooms through one system, sports through
another, run by a different department.

I originally claimed no system helps when a request fails.
Also false - MIDAS suggests alternatives. But they come from
a substitution table an administrator maintains by hand.
There's no ranking, nothing that weighs group size against
room size, and nothing that learns.

On conversational access - MIDAS shipped an MCP server in
July, so an AI assistant can book through it. But it's an
admin add-on on one shared account with a confidential key.
A student can't touch it.

So the gap is: nothing spans facility types, nothing RANKS
alternatives or learns from them, and no student gets
conversational access to live availability under their own
permissions.


------------------------------------------------------------
THE DESIGN
------------------------------------------------------------

Architecture is one Next.js application serving three role
dashboards, Supabase for data and authentication, the
scoring engine, and a language model behind a swappable
wrapper.

THE TWO-LEVEL FACILITY MODEL is the contribution. A category
- Badminton, Discussion Room - carries the venue and six
rule parameters. Individual courts inherit them and store
only what differs: name, capacity, status. An optional
per-room override where any null column inherits the
category value. So an administrator can create a swimming
pool with its own rules and no code is deployed.

CONFLICT PREVENTION is the part I'm proudest of, and it's
provable. I don't check for clashes in my code. I declare it
to PostgreSQL as an exclusion constraint over the facility
and the time range. If two students submit at the same
instant, the database rejects one. There is no race to lose.
Every reviewed system checks in application code, which can
lose it.

Row-level security does the same for access: a manager can't
read another manager's facilities even calling the API
directly.

THE RECOMMENDER. Six equations. Three feasibility
constraints - opening hours, duration limits, capacity and
advance window. One overlap predicate using interval
algebra, which is the constraint I just described. Then a
scoring function:

    S = w1.T + w2.C + w3.U

Time proximity, capacity fit, off-peak load. And a sixth
equation for the learning step - accepted suggestions shift
the weights by a rate of 0.05, renormalised, stored per
student.

A worked example. A student wants a room for two at three
o'clock. It's taken. Two o'clock scores 0.847. One o'clock
scores 0.645. Two o'clock wins even though both are
adjacent, because one o'clock falls in a peak period. Raise
the group to six and two o'clock lifts to 0.938 - the same
slot scores differently for a different request.

That capacity term is my answer to Sunway. Their system can
only allow or refuse, so to stop pairs taking ten-seat pods
they wrote a hard minimum - which also blocks a legitimate
pair when the small pods are full. Mine ranks, so it steers
the pair to the six-seater without forbidding them the large
one.

THE CHATBOT is scoped to four question families. The model
calls a typed function, the server runs it under the
student's own row-level security context, and the model
composes its answer from the returned rows only. Outside
those four families it deflects to staff. A narrow bot
that's right beats a broad one that guesses.


------------------------------------------------------------
PROFESSIONAL PRACTICES
------------------------------------------------------------

PDPA compliance - minimal collection, bounded retention,
access enforced in the database. OWASP for security, WCAG
2.1 AA for accessibility.

BEM code 2.1.4 on competence, and 2.1.7 on not expressing
opinions unfounded on fact - which is precisely why the
chatbot can't guess. That's a professional obligation
expressed as an architecture.

Public health and safety through occupancy limits, which
encode a fire-safety rule and could impose a health capacity
cap without a code change.

Whole-life cost: hosted, so no hardware to buy, power, cool
or dispose of. Net zero through eliminating paper, removing
counter journeys, and using no-show data to stop
conditioning rooms that are booked and empty.

Continuous professional development - three technologies I
learned independently during Phase 1, from primary sources,
verified by testing. One of the models I depended on was
deprecated mid-project, which is why the design assumes its
own components will be superseded.


------------------------------------------------------------
MANAGEMENT AND COST
------------------------------------------------------------

Fortnightly supervisor meetings, logged.

Recurring cost is zero - the whole stack runs on free tiers,
with a small contingency. Total estimate 225 ringgit, mostly
printing.

Commercially, the rule engine is the interesting part:
policy is configuration, so another institution deploys the
same build. Per-institution subscription, near-zero marginal
cost. Private colleges first - shorter procurement.

Phase 1 was managed by front-loading. The system is built,
so Phase 2 is evaluation rather than implementation. Two
items slipped and are carried in the logs: ethics submission
and Turnitin.


------------------------------------------------------------
WHERE IT STANDS, AND WHAT I CAN'T YET CLAIM
------------------------------------------------------------

Complete: the literature review, the concept design, a
working prototype with all three roles' features, and
sixteen unit tests on the recommendation engine, all
passing.

I can demonstrate a booking with an AI suggestion in about
thirty seconds if that's useful.

    [Offer the demo here. It is the strongest evidence
     you have.]

Three honest limitations.

Recommendation quality is untested with real students until
ethics clears. That's the critical path and my biggest risk.

My peak-hour windows are hardcoded rather than learned from
booking history, even though I now have the data to learn
them.

And "one system across every facility type" is proven in the
schema but demonstrated on seven facilities, not a whole
campus - so I'd say the design removes the barrier, not that
I've shown it at scale.

I'd also flag one argument rather than a finding. I say
online booking removed the friction that limited hoarding. I
can evidence that hoarding happens inside modern systems. I
can't evidence that it got WORSE than under paper, because
nobody has measured that.

Happy to take questions.


============================================================
IF YOU ONLY REMEMBER FIVE THINGS
============================================================

1. Digitising the queue solved the queue, not the
   allocation.

2. Sunway runs a modern system and still needs a person at
   a desk to check you turned up.

3. Booking policy lives in the database as configuration,
   not written into the program.

4. Double-booking isn't checked for. It's impossible - the
   database rejects it.

5. Nothing on the market ranks the alternatives it offers,
   and nothing learns from what you pick.
